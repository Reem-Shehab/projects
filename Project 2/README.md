## Problem Statement
A philanthropic foundation wants to design development programs that work, but it has no clear blueprint to follow. This project uses Gapminder data from 1960 to 2020 to find the countries that moved from low income and short lives to high income and long lives, so the foundation can copy what worked.

## Executive Summary
The foundation needs proven models of development. To find them I used four Gapminder datasets, population, life expectancy, GNI per capita, and fertility, for every country from 1960 to 2020. The files came in a wide shape with numbers stored as text like `3.28M`, so I reshaped them to long form, turned the text into real numbers, merged them on country and year, and kept the years 1960 to 2020. The clean table has 190 countries and 11,584 rows.

The main finding is that the full leap is rare. Using clear thresholds, poor and short-lived in 1960 and rich and long-lived by 2020, only three countries made it: South Korea, China, and Oman. Their income grew 22.9 to 25.6 times, while the world median income only went from 1345 to 4785. Income and life expectancy stayed tightly linked, with a correlation of 0.82 in 2020. Splitting countries into developed and underdeveloped groups showed that health converged but wealth diverged: the poorer group gained more years of life, 19.4 against 12.1, while the income gap grew wider. As countries developed, population growth slowed and families had fewer children, from about 6 to under 2.

For the foundation, the lesson is to fund health first because it is cheap and works almost anywhere, invest in girls' education and family planning to speed the transition, and copy the Korea model of industry plus education where a country is ready. Health can reach everyone, but raising income is the harder and slower job.

## Data and Data Dictionary
The data files are packaged in `Data.zip`. Unzip it in this folder to create a `Data/` folder before running the notebook.

Source: Gapminder (https://www.gapminder.org). The fertility file is based on the UN World Population Prospects 2024, and GNI per capita traces back to the World Bank Atlas method.

The cleaned dataset (`world_development_clean.csv`) has these columns:

| Feature | Type | Source | Description |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| country | object | Original | Country name. |
| year | int64 | Original | Calendar year, 1960 to 2020. |
| decade | int64 | Engineered | Decade the year falls in, so 1994 becomes 1990. |
| population | float64 | Original | Number of people, parsed from text like 3.28M. |
| life_expectancy | float64 | Original | Expected years of life at birth. |
| gni_per_capita | float64 | Original | Gross national income per person, 2021 USD, Atlas method. |
| pop_growth | float64 | Engineered | Yearly percent change in a country's population. |
| fertility | float64 | Added file | Children per woman (total fertility rate). |

## Conclusions and Recommendations
Key findings:
- The full transition from poor and short-lived to rich and long-lived was rare. Only 3 of 190 countries did it: South Korea, China, and Oman.
- Those three grew income 22.9 to 25.6 times, far above the world median (1345 to 4785).
- Health converged but wealth diverged. The underdeveloped group gained 19.4 years of life, more than the developed group's 12.1, while the income gap grew.
- As countries developed, fertility fell from about 6 to under 2 children per woman, and population growth slowed toward zero.
- Income and life expectancy are still tightly linked in 2020, correlation 0.82.

Recommendations for the foundation:
- Fund low-cost health first, such as vaccination, clean water, and clinics. It adds years of life fast and reaches poor countries too.
- Invest in girls' schooling and family planning, which drive the fall in fertility and the whole transition.
- Copy the Korea model of export industry plus mass education, the most durable path.
- Help oil economies like Oman diversify into industry and skills, since oil income is volatile.
- Target the poorest countries first, where each dollar buys the most years of life.

## Areas for Further Research
- The older years mix records with Gapminder estimates, so early values are less certain.
- GNI per capita is a mean and hides inequality inside a country.
- A few small states dropped out of the merge because they were missing from one file.
- Adding education levels or health spending would help explain why these countries moved and give a clearer picture of how the transition happened.
